\section{Executive Summary}\label{executive-summary}

SEPA's flood inundation models assume every extreme flow event has the same shape, scaled only by its peak flow. In reality, events differ in how quickly river flow rises, how long it stays high and how slowly it falls, so this assumption gives an incomplete picture of flood risk. As inundation models are slow to run, SEPA plans to approximate them with fast statistical models, known as emulators, which need each hydrograph (the record of river flow through an event) to be described by only a few numbers.

Using 25 years of data from the River Don at Parkhill, we identified 118 extreme flow events and compared SEPA's fixed shape with five ways of describing hydrograph shape: polynomial regression, principal component analysis (PCA), wavelets, B-splines and a gamma-type function. Each was judged on how accurately it reproduced events it had not been fitted to, particularly their peak flow, timing and volume.

PCA and wavelets performed best, reducing the typical reconstruction error from 47.9 \(\text{m}^3/\text{s}\) under SEPA's approach to 4.46 and 3.34 \(\text{m}^3/\text{s}\) respectively. PCA captures 95\% of the variation in shape with 6 numbers, the largest single source (59\%) being how long flow stays high, not just how high it peaks.

We recommend that SEPA adopts PCA. It needs 9 emulator inputs against 16 for wavelets, so fewer inundation model runs are needed, and fewer of its simulated hydrographs are physically unrealistic (14\% rise above their peak, against 30\% for wavelets). SEPA's fixed shape overstates the volume of water by 84\% on average, and gives a single volume of 54.3 million \(\text{m}^3\) for an event of median size and timing; with uncertainty in shape included, such an event could hold between 22.4 and 45.0 million \(\text{m}^3\).

PCA does not fully reproduce sharp or multi-peak events, so its simulated 90\% ranges contain only 86\% of observed flows. Even so, combined with an emulator, it lets SEPA explore a much wider range of plausible extreme flow events, supporting better-informed decisions on flood defences, land use and development.

\subsection{Standardising the hydrographs}\label{standardising-the-hydrographs}

To isolate variation in hydrograph shape, we standardise each extreme flow event as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Events are defined between rising and falling limb troughs.
\item
  Time is rescaled to the interval {[}0,1{]}, with zero and one representing event start and end respectively.
\item
  Relative peak position is calculated as \(\frac{\text{peak time} - \text{event start}}{\text{event duration}}\).
\item
  Rising and falling limbs are rescaled separately to align all hydrograph peaks at \(u^* =\) 0.38 so that peak timing doesn't dominate the analysis.
\item
  Discharge is divided by maximum flow, standardising hydrograph peaks to have value of one.
\item
  Hydrographs are reconstructed using linear interpolation between 64 equally spaced, common points on the standardised time scale.
\item
  The resulting data are arranged into a matrix (\(Y\)), with one row per event and 64 columns for each time point.
\end{itemize}

Peak discharge, event duration and relative peak position are retained to reverse standardisation during validation.

\section{Methods for representing hydrograph shape}\label{methods-for-representing-hydrograph-shape}

Each standardised event (Section \ref{standardising-the-hydrographs}) comprises of 64 discharge values, peak discharge \(Q_i^{\text{peak}}\), duration \(W_i\), and relative peak position \(\tau_i\). To reduce the number of emulator inputs, we investigate five different methods: polynomial regression, principal component analysis (PCA), wavelets, B-splines and a gamma-type function.

Most methods approximate each standardised hydrograph by a weighted sum of \(K\) basis functions,

\begin{equation}
y_i(u) \approx \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_{ik}\,\phi_k(u),
\label{eq:basis}
\end{equation}

where \(\phi_k(u)\) are the basis functions and \(\theta_{ik}\) are the shape parameters for event \(i\). The methods differ mainly in how these functions are chosen. The exceptions are PCA, which first subtracts the mean curve before applying \eqref{eq:basis}, and the gamma-type curve, which is a nonlinear function of its parameters (\citeproc{ref-nash1957instantaneous}{Nash, 1957}).

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_i = \left(Q_i^{\text{peak}},\; W_i,\; \tau_i,\; \boldsymbol{\theta}_i\right).
\label{eq:inputs}
\end{equation}

Peak discharge, duration and relative peak position are retained separately, giving (\(K+3\)) inputs in total to the emulator, where \(K\) is considerably smaller than the previous 64.

\subsection{SEPA's Approach}\label{method-SEPA}

Our baseline is SEPA's fixed shape, where the standardised hydrographs are averaged without peak alignment,

\begin{equation}
\bar{y}(u) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N} \tilde{y}_l(u),
\label{eq:sepa}
\end{equation}

and rescaled to have a peak value of one,

\begin{equation}
\hat{y}(u) = \frac{\bar{y}(u)}{\max_u \bar{y}(u)}.
\label{eq:sepa-2}
\end{equation}

The fitted hydrograph for event \(i\) is stretched to duration \(W_i\) and multiplied by peak discharge \(Q_i^{\text{peak}}\).
Unlike SEPA's current approach, our baseline allows duration to vary, potentially improving its reconstruction accuracy.

\subsection{Evaluation criteria}\label{evaluation-criteria}

We compare methods on accuracy, parsimony and fitness for purpose (\citeproc{ref-wilson2026lec5}{Wilson, 2026c}), both to choose each method's number of parameters (Sections \ref{method-polynomial}--\ref{method-other}) and in Section \ref{Comparison}.

\textbf{Accuracy:} measures how closely each method reconstructs the observed hydrographs using the root mean squared error (RMSE):

\begin{equation}
\text{RMSE}_i = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{64}\textstyle\sum_{j}\big(\hat{Q}_i(t_j) - Q_i(t_j)\big)^2}.
\label{eq:rmse}
\end{equation}

Errors are calculated on the original discharge and time scales as this is what the flood inundation model uses.

For methods estimating a shared shape or basis (SEPA's approach, PCA and the wavelet coefficient selection), leave-one-out validation refits this representation using the remaining \(N - 1\) events.

For methods where each event is fitted separately (polynomial, B-spline and gamma-type), leaving an event out does not change its fit, so their errors are in-sample; we report them over all 118 events.

\textbf{Parsimony:} The number of shape parameters and their correlations across events was compared, as fewer parameters reduce complexity, provided that reconstruction accuracy is maintained, while strong correlations complicate uncertainty modelling.

\textbf{Fitness for purpose:} The \texttt{RMSE} measures overall reconstruction accuracy, but it doesn't determine whether the method represents a valid range of hydrograph shapes. So, we also measure errors in peak discharge, peak timing and total flow volume (\citeproc{ref-wilson2026lec5}{Wilson, 2026c}):

\begin{align}
\text{Peak error}_i &= \max_j \hat{Q}_i(t_j) - \max_j Q_i(t_j), \label{eq:peak}\\
\text{Timing error}_i &= \hat{t}_{i}^{\,\text{peak}} - t_{i}^{\,\text{peak}}, \label{eq:timing}\\
\text{Volume error}_i &= 100 \times \frac{\int \hat{Q}_i(t)\,dt - \int Q_i(t)\,dt}{\int Q_i(t)\,dt}\ \%, \label{eq:volume}
\end{align}

Volumes use the trapezium rule. As every method except the baseline aligns peaks, timing error mainly assesses the baseline.

\subsection{Polynomial regression}\label{method-polynomial}

The first method uses polynomial regression of a degree-p polynomial in time to approximate each standardised hydrograph:
\begin{equation}
y_i(u_j) \approx \theta_{i0} + \theta_{i1}u_j + \theta_{i2}u_j^2 + \dots + \theta_{ip}u_j^p, \qquad j = 1, \dots, 64.
\label{eq:poly}
\end{equation}

For each event, the \(p+1\) coefficients were estimated by ordinary least squares,
\begin{equation}
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_i = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}_i
\label{eq:OLS}
\end{equation}

where \(X\) is the 64\(\times(p+1)\) design matrix with columns \(1,u,\ldots,u^p\), and \(\mathbf{y}_i\) contains the 64 standardised discharge values. These coefficients form the shape parameters of each event.

To choose the degree \(p\), we tested degrees 2-15, comparing errors across all events (Figure \ref{fig:polychoice}). We also examined the significance of the highest-order coefficient (Table \ref{tab:polysig}). However, these tests are less reliable as neighbouring discharge readings are correlated, violating the independence assumption used to calculate standard errors.

\subsection{Principal component analysis (PCA)}\label{method-pca}

PCA estimates basis functions directly from the observed hydrographs (\citeproc{ref-wilson2026lec4}{Wilson, 2026b}).

The standardised hydrographs are arranged in a 118 \(\times\) 64 matrix \(Y\), with one row per event, and centred each column by subtracting the mean curve \(\bar{y}(u_j)\). The principle components \(\phi_1, \phi_2, \dots\), are the eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrix, ordered by explained variance. Each hydrograph is then approximated by

\begin{equation}
y_i(u_j) \approx \bar{y}(u_j) + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \xi_{ik}\,\phi_k(u_j), \qquad \xi_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^{64} \big(y_i(u_j) - \bar{y}(u_j)\big)\,\phi_k(u_j),
\label{eq:pca}
\end{equation}

The scores \(\xi_{i1}, \dots, \xi_{iK}\) are the shape parameters for event \(i\). Unlike polynomial coefficients, they are uncorrelated by construction, simplifying uncertainty modelling.

The first three principal components explain 86\% of the variation (Figure \ref{fig:pcaloadings}). PC1 (59\%) mainly describes event width, PC2 contrasts the rising and falling limbs, and PC3 captures the difference in the steepness of the rising limb.

We select \(K\) using cumulative variance explained (Figure \ref{fig:pcacumvar}) and reconstruction errors (Figure \ref{fig:pcachoice}). Six components explain approximately 95\% of the variation, with little improvement in leave-one-out RMSE from adding a seventh. We therefore selected \(K=6\), reducing each hydrograph to six shape parameters.

\subsection{Wavelets}\label{method-wavelets}

Wavelets represent a hydrograph using functions localised in time and scale, making them useful for capturing sharp peaks and smaller fluctuations without changing the whole fitted curve (\citeproc{ref-wilson2026lec4}{Wilson, 2026b}). We applied the discrete wavelet transformation (DWT) to each standardised hydrograph, separating it into a smooth component and details at three resolution levels:

\begin{equation}
y_i(u) = \sum_{k} a_{ik}\,\varphi_k(u) + \sum_{j=1}^{3}\sum_{k} d_{ijk}\,\psi_{jk}(u),
\label{eq:wavelet}
\end{equation}

where \(a_{ik}\) are the smooth coefficients and \(d_{ijk}\) are the detail coefficients at scale \(j\) and location \(k\). At 3 levels, each 64-point hydrograph gives 8 smooth and 56 detail coefficients, allowing exact reconstruction if all are retained.

We retain all 8 smooth coefficients and the \(M\) detail coefficient positions with the largest mean absolute values across events, ensuring consistent parameter definitions.

We selected \(M\) by comparing in-sample and leave-one-out reconstruction errors (Figure \ref{fig:wavchoice}). Peak error fell sharply up to \(M = 5\), with little improvement thereafter, while \texttt{RMSE} decreased more gradually. We therefore retained five detail coefficients, giving 13 shape parameters.

\subsection{Other methods}\label{method-other}

As well as the three suggested methods we also considered two further methods, each addressing a weakness found above.

\subsubsection{B-splines}\label{method-bspline}

Unlike a single polynomial, B-splines represent a curve using smoothly joined polynomial pieces, allowing for local changes without affecting the entire hydrograph (\citeproc{ref-ramsay2005functional}{Ramsay \& Silverman, 2005}). We fitted cubic B-splines with equally spaced knots to each standardised event:

\begin{equation}
y_i(u_j) \approx \theta_{i0} + \sum_{l=1}^{L} \theta_{il}\,B_l(u_j)
\label{eq:bspline}
\end{equation}

where \(B_l(u_j)\) are the spline basis functions and \(\theta_{il}\) are estimated by least squares. We chose \(L =\) 8, giving 9 fitted coefficients, close to the 8 used in polynomial regression, allowing comparison at similar complexity.

\subsubsection{Gamma-type function}\label{method-gamma}

Following Nash, (\citeproc{ref-nash1957instantaneous}{Nash, 1957}), we fit a gamma-type hydrograph with additional baseflow terms using a nonlinear least squares. Its three interpretable parameters describe relative baseflow, peak timing and peak sharpness. Although parsimonious, the function is restricted to single-peak events.

\section{Uncertainty analysis}\label{uncertainty}

Figure \ref{fig:param-qq} suggests that the residuals are approximately Normal through the centre of their distributions, but that several depart from Normality in the tails. In particular, PC2 and PC4-PC6 have heavy lower tails, while for the wavelets, S1-S3 are right-skewed and D1, D2, D4 and D5 have heavy tails, particularly in the upper tail. We therefore fit a conditional multivariate Normal distribution alongside a conditional smoothed bootstrap, which does not assume normality.

\subsection{Statistical model and sampling scheme}\label{model}

Let \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_i = (\theta_{i1}, \dots, \theta_{iK})^\top\) be the shape parameters for event \(i\), either the PCA scores (\(K = 6\)) or the wavelet coefficients (\(K = 13\)), and \(\mathbf{s}_i = (Q_i^{\text{peak}}, W_i, \tau_i)^\top\) its standardisation parameters. Both approaches regress the shape parameters on the standardisation parameters,

\[
\boldsymbol{\theta}_i = \boldsymbol{\beta}_0 + B\,\mathbf{s}_i + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_i,
\]

where \(B\) is a \(K \times 3\) matrix of coefficients, and fitting this by least squares gives residuals \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_1, \dots, \hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_N\) with sample covariance \(\hat{\Sigma}\). A new event with standardisation parameters \(\mathbf{s}^\ast\) then has simulated shape parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\ast = \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_0 + \hat{B}\,\mathbf{s}^\ast + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\ast\), assuming constant residual spread for every \(\mathbf{s}^\ast\) and differ only in how \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\ast\) is generated. Our statistical model, the conditional Normal, takes

\[
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\ast \sim N_K(\mathbf{0}, \hat{\Sigma}),
\]

while our sampling scheme, the conditional smoothed bootstrap, takes

\[
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^\ast = \frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_J + h\,\mathbf{z}}{\sqrt{1+h^2}}, \qquad J \sim \text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,N\}, \quad \mathbf{z} \sim N_K(\mathbf{0}, \hat{\Sigma}),
\]

with \(h = \big(4/((K+2)N)\big)^{1/(K+4)}\) (\citeproc{ref-silverman1986}{Silverman, 1986}). The bootstrap retains some of the heavy tails seen in Section \ref{distribution-and-dependencies-of-the-shape-parameters}, although the Normal noise accounts for 25\% (PCA) and 33\% (wavelets) of the variance of the simulated residuals.

Finally, we turn \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\ast\) into a curve using Equation \eqref{eq:pca} for PCA or \eqref{eq:wavelet} for wavelets, and reverse the standardisation with \(\mathbf{s}^\ast\).

\subsection{Evaluation criteria}\label{uncertainty-criteria}

To evaluate how well each approach simulates extreme flow events, we simulate \(M =\) 200 shapes for each event \(i\) using its own standardisation parameters \(\mathbf{s}_i\) and convert them to hydrographs \(\hat{Q}_i^{(m)}(t_j)\). We then compare these with the observed hydrograph \(Q_i(t_j)\) at the 64 time points of Section \ref{standardising-the-hydrographs}, averaging each criterion over all events.

\textbf{Coverage:} the percentage of the observed hydrograph lying inside the simulated 90\% band,

\[
\text{Coverage}_i = \frac{100}{64}\sum_{j} \mathbf{1}\big\{L_i(t_j) \le Q_i(t_j) \le U_i(t_j)\big\},
\]

where \(L_i(t_j)\) and \(U_i(t_j)\) are the 5\% and 95\% quantiles of the simulated flows at \(t_j\).

\textbf{CRPS:} a measure that rewards simulations that are both accurate and appropriately spread (\citeproc{ref-gneitingraftery2007}{Gneiting \& Raftery, 2007}),

\[
\text{CRPS}_i = \frac{1}{64}\sum_{j}\left( \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m} \big|\hat{Q}_i^{(m)}(t_j) - Q_i(t_j)\big| - \frac{1}{2M^2}\sum_{m}\sum_{m'} \big|\hat{Q}_i^{(m)}(t_j) - \hat{Q}_i^{(m')}(t_j)\big| \right),
\]

in \(\text{m}^3/\text{s}\), where lower is better. For SEPA's single fixed shape, the second term is zero and the CRPS reduces to the mean absolute error, allowing a direct comparison.

\textbf{Physical validity:} the percentage of simulated standardised curves that fall below zero or rise more than 1\% above the peak value of one, neither of which a real hydrograph can do.

\section{Recommendation and emulator application}\label{recommendation-and-emulator-application}

\subsection{Recommendation}\label{recommendation}

We recommend that SEPA represents hydrograph shape using PCA with 6 principal component scores, alongside peak flow, duration and peak position, and simulates new shapes using the conditional smoothed bootstrap scheme of Section \ref{model}.

PCA offers substantial improvement on SEPA's current approach, reducing the held-out RMSE by 91\% and its volume error from 84.5\% to 0.37\% (Table \ref{tab:cmp-main}). Wavelets reconstruct the observed events slightly more accurately, but comparing at the same number of shape parameters (\(K = 13\)) the PCA is more accurate, so their advantage comes from extra parameters rather than a better basis. Hence, PCA needs only 9 emulator inputs compared with 16 for wavelets, substantially reducing the number of inundation model runs required, and its joint distribution needs 21 correlations estimated rather than 78 for wavelets.

When simulating new extreme flow events, both methods are almost identical in terms of coverage and reduce the CRPS of SEPA's fixed shape by at least 76\%, but PCA's curves are more physically valid, with 14\% rising above their peak compared with 30\% for wavelets. Our analysis also shows that an event of median size and timing could hold between 22.4 and 45.0 million \(\text{m}^3\) of water depending on its shape alone, whereas SEPA's fixed shape gives a single volume of 54.3 million \(\text{m}^3\), which lies above the 95th percentile of every scheme we considered (Table \ref{tab:volumes}).

The bootstrap and conditional Normal gave almost identical coverage and CRPS, but we prefer the bootstrap as it produces fewer curves with negative flow (5.4\% against 7.4\%) and does not assume Normality, given the heavy tails in Figure \ref{fig:param-qq}.

However, the method has limitations, particularly that despite being better than wavelets, it still produces 5.4\% of curves with negative flow and 14\% rising above their peak. We would recommend that SEPA discards any physically invalid curve and draws a replacement. Further, the 6 components do not fully capture multi-peak events, so the uncertainty in shape is likely to be slightly understated, which contributes to the coverage of 86.4\% falling below the 90\% we sought. Finally, the bootstrap resamples residuals from only 118 events, so it cannot generate shapes that are much more extreme than those we have observed, which may miss out on the rarest and most impactful events.

\subsection{Emulator application}\label{emulator}

Our recommended method describes each extreme flow event by 9 numbers, which become the emulator's inputs and give a representation that is now suitably low-dimensional for emulation.

\textbf{Collecting the data to fit the emulator:} SEPA first selects a few hundred combinations of the 9 inputs using an experimental design, such as a grid over their plausible ranges (\citeproc{ref-wilson2026lec3}{Wilson, 2026a}). Each combination is turned into a hydrograph using Equation \eqref{eq:pca}, with the standardisation then reversed, and each hydrograph is run through the flood inundation model so that the outputs of interest can be recorded. An emulator is fitted to these pairs of inputs and outputs and checked against runs held back from fitting.

\textbf{Using the emulator to explore uncertainty in flood inundation:} SEPA can then use our conditional smoothed bootstrap to simulate new extreme flow events, in practice drawing peak flows from its existing extreme value distribution rather than resampling observed events as we did. As the emulator is quick to evaluate, thousands of these events can be passed through it to give a full distribution of flood depths or extents that reflects uncertainty in both the size and the shape of the hydrograph. The emulator's function uncertainty (\citeproc{ref-wilson2026lec3}{Wilson, 2026a}) should also be included, along with any remaining structural discrepancy, both between the inundation model and real flooding and between our statistical model and real hydrograph shapes. The latter is shown in our simulated 90\% bands containing only around 86\% of the observed hydrographs.
